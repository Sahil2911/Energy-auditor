## Design logic

Three facts shaped this plan.

**1. You are an electrical engineer, and Paper 4 is mostly thermal.** Of the 15
Book-4 chapters, nine are thermal or thermal-adjacent — boilers, furnaces,
cogeneration, heat exchangers, HVAC, power plants, steel, cement, textile. Your
electrical strengths (motors, VSDs, pumps, fans, compressors) cover maybe four.
So the plan spends **Days 1–7 on thermal** and compresses electrical and fluid
systems into **Days 8–10**, where you will move faster.

**2. You cleared Papers 1, 2 and 3.** You are not starting from zero on thermal
theory — you already passed Paper 2. What Paper 4 adds is *assessment under
messy, incomplete, real-plant data*. So the lessons skip basic derivations and
go straight to the assessment methods and the numerical patterns.

**3. Section III is 80% of the paper.** Every day ends with at least one full
20-mark question under time. Reading theory without writing solutions is the
most common way to fail this paper.

## Teaching standard

Set after Day 2 feedback. Every lesson must:

1. **Explain the physics before the formula.** No formula appears without what is
   physically happening and why it takes that form. Constants get derived, not
   asserted — 11.6 comes from 32/12 ÷ 0.23, and saying so is faster than
   memorising it.
2. **Cite its source.** Each day names the BEE chapter behind it, with a link
   where the chapter is publicly downloadable. See `reference/resources.md`.
3. **Assume nothing.** Clearing Paper 2 proves a pass mark on thermal utilities,
   not depth. Where a topic underpins Paper 4, it gets taught properly regardless
   of what was assumed known.
4. **Connect back.** Each concept links to where it reappears — the 44/12 in the
   flue gas formula is the 3.67 in the CO₂ calculation, and saying so once
   replaces memorising twice.
5. **Depth in proportion to weight.** A chapter appearing in 7 of 8 sittings gets
   the engineering behind the formulas — what the quantities physically are, what
   dominates, what an auditor would measure — not just a calculation recipe. If a
   lesson reduces to "here are the formulas", it is unfinished.
7. **Build to the formula, never open with it.** Start from what the equipment is
   and what physically happens inside it. Introduce each symbol only when the
   physics demands it, and **derive** the non-obvious results rather than quoting
   them — the LMTD is an integration, not a definition, and seeing that is the
   difference between recall and understanding. A rung that opens with an
   unexplained equation is a rung written backwards.
6. **Check prerequisites before setting a question.** See the Reviewer framework.
   Name the reachable parts, or pick another question.

## Non-negotiable daily rhythm

For a 3.5-hour day:

| Block | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| A | 45 min | New concept — read the lesson, follow the worked example |
| B | 60 min | Guided practice — solve past questions with the solution covered |
| C | 60 min | **Timed 20-mark question**, closed notes except guidebooks |
| D | 25 min | Mark yourself against the model answer; log errors |

Block D is not optional. The error log is what the Reviewer reads.

## Standing risks

- **Thermal depth.** Biggest risk. Mitigated by front-loading and by the Day 7
  checkpoint, which is early enough to re-plan around.
- **Open-book overconfidence.** The books do not answer the questions; they hold
  the property tables. Candidates lose time hunting. Mitigated by building the
  index from Day 1, not Day 15.
- **The 2.5-hour change.** Unknown extra content. Mitigated by practising at
  2-hour pace so there is slack.
- **Scanned 21st/22nd papers.** Two sittings currently unusable. Worth
  recovering as extra mocks during the buffer.
