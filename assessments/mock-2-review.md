## Section I — 6/10

**Q1 — "The lower the dew point of air, the higher is the moisture."** You said
True; it is **False**. Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated.
Drier air must be cooled *further* before it condenses, so a **lower dew point
means less moisture**. The two run together, not opposite.

**Q4 — FD fan flow vs ID fan on balanced draft.** You said Higher; it is **Lower**.
Follow the gas: the FD fan pushes only combustion **air**. The ID fan pulls that
same air **plus** the mass added by the fuel, **plus** any air in-leakage through
the casing, **and at 150–200 °C rather than ambient**, so the volume is larger
again. The ID fan is always the bigger machine.

**Q6 — "Lower the TTD in a steam condenser, ______ is the heat transfer rate."**
You said Lower; it is **Higher**. TTD is the gap between the condensing steam and
the cooling water leaving. Squeezing that gap means more heat crossed for the same
driving temperature — a *better* condenser. Same direction as a cooling tower's
approach: **small approach = good tower**, small TTD = good condenser.

**Q9 — "Oxygen and nitrogen in the fuel do not contribute to calorific value."**
You said False; it is **True**. Only **C, H and S** burn. Fuel-bound oxygen is
already oxidised — it contributes nothing, and in fact *reduces* the air needed,
which is exactly why theoretical air carries the term:

```
    A_th = [11.6 C + 34.8 (H₂ − O₂/8) + 4.35 S]/100
                            ↑ the fuel's own oxygen, subtracted
```

**You have used that minus sign correctly since Day 2A.** Q9 is the same fact
stated in words.

> **Three of these four are direction questions** — dew point, fan sizes, TTD. All
> three are settled by asking *which way does the physical quantity move?* rather
> than recalling a sentence. That is the same family as the heat rate inversion
> from Day 12.

```
   Your line:  60 × 826 × 2.223 × 4.18 × (230 − 210)  = 92,10,369 kCal/hr
   Correct:    60 × 826 × (2.223/4.186) × 20          =  5,26,383 kCal/hr
```

**Cp is given in kJ/kg°C. You multiplied by 4.18 where you had to divide.** The
answer came out **17.5× too large** — a kilogram of thermic fluid cannot absorb
more heat than the same kilogram of water.

**The reason your recommendation survived** is that the error is a common factor.
Both fuels were sized on the same inflated load, so the ratio between them held and
briquettes still won. **The method earned its marks; the conversion lost two.**

> ### The check that catches this every time
>
> **A kJ number is always the bigger one.** 1 kCal = 4.186 kJ, so:
>
> ```
>     kJ → kCal :  DIVIDE by 4.186     (the number gets smaller)
>     kCal → kJ :  MULTIPLY by 4.186   (the number gets bigger)
> ```
>
> And the anchor: **water is 1 kCal/kg°C = 4.186 kJ/kg°C.** A thermic fluid at
> 2.223 kJ/kg°C is *about half of water*, so in kCal it must be **about 0.53** —
> never 9.3. One glance at 2.223 against 4.186 settles the direction.
>
> This is the twelfth member of the reciprocal family and the first unit-system one.

## N-4(B) — building cooling load, ≈16/20

**(a) 29.595 kW, (b) 54.241 kW, (c) 83.836 kW — all exact.** The eight-row table,
the CLF applied to sensible gains only, the ballast-free lighting term, the latent
infiltration at Δh = 14.7. Day 13B was read once and never drilled, and it came
back whole.

**(d) is the one loss, and it is a dimensional slip.** You wrote the right sentence:

> *"Additional heat gain will be same as existing 42,841.143"*

**That is correct** — doubling the air change rate from 2 to 4 doubles the
infiltration load, so the *increment* equals the existing figure, 42.84 kW. Then
the next line went:

```
   ∝ [(1210 × 2) + (3010 × 2)] = 8440  →  8.44 kW
```

**1210 and 3010 are not watts.** They are `ρCp` in J/m³·K and `ρh_fg` in J per m³
per g/kg — they only become watts after multiplying by a flow in m³/s **and** a
temperature or humidity difference:

```
   Q = 1210 × V̇ × ΔT   +   3010 × V̇ × Δ(g/kg)
```

Multiplying them by the air change number alone drops both the volume flow and the
driving differences. The figure you already had — 42,841 W — was the answer.

```
   additional cooling load                              = 42.8 kW
   additional electrical = 42.8/(COP 3.5 × motor 0.95)  = 12.88 kW
   cost = 12.88 × 8 h/day × 300 days × ₹7.5/kWh /10⁵    = ₹2.32 lakh/year
```

You reached ₹1.519 lakh. **Your hours, days and tariff were all right** — the chain
after the slip is arithmetically clean. Only the load entering it was wrong.

> **The check:** before using any constant, say what its units are out loud. If
> `1210 × 2` is to be watts, then 1210 must be watts per air change — and it plainly
> is not, because the same 1210 appeared four lines earlier multiplied by a flow and
> a ΔT.
